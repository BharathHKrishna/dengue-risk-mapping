Project explainer

Can satellites predict dengue, even in places the model has never seen?

A plain-language guide to the whole project: what it does, why it is built this way, every concept and metric, and what the results really mean. No prior knowledge of epidemiology or machine learning needed.

Live map  |  Code on GitHub

1. The short version

I built a system that takes free satellite and weather data (temperature, rain, humidity, vegetation, buildings, population), combines it with reported dengue cases for every town in Brazil, and trains a model to predict how much dengue each place will have next month.

Then I asked the hard question: does the model still work in places it never saw during training? I tested this three ways: on future years, on whole regions of Brazil it never saw, and on a different country (India).

The one-sentence lesson Climate tells you when dengue season comes, but not how bad a year will be, and a model can look fine in one country and fail in another. You only find this out if you test on places the model has never seen.

2. Why this project

Health departments need to know where and when dengue outbreaks are likely, so they can plan mosquito control, public awareness campaigns, and hospital beds ahead of time.

Many countries do not have good, detailed case records. But satellite and weather data exist everywhere on Earth, for free. So the dream is: learn the link between climate and dengue in a place with great data, then apply it where data is poor.

The danger: a model that works in Brazil might quietly fail in India, and nobody notices until decisions have been made. So this project is less about building the most accurate model, and more about honestly measuring when a model can be trusted in a new place.

Why Brazil?

Brazil publishes weekly dengue counts for every single municipality through InfoDengue. That is rare, detailed, open data: perfect for training and checking a model.

Why India?

India has a big dengue burden but only publishes yearly totals per state. That makes it a realistic, hard test: can a model trained elsewhere rank Indian states correctly?

Why satellites?

They give the same type of measurement for every place on Earth, every month. My master's thesis built exactly this kind of geospatial pipeline, so I reused that skill for health.

3. Dengue in 2 minutes

Dengue is a virus spread by Aedes mosquitoes. The mosquito bites an infected person, the virus grows inside the mosquito, and then the mosquito bites someone else.

Weather matters at every step:

Temperature

Warmer means mosquitoes grow faster and the virus multiplies faster. But too hot is bad for them too. Lab studies put the sweet spot around 26 to 29 degrees C.

Rain

Rain fills buckets, tires and gutters with standing water, where mosquitoes lay eggs.

Humidity

Mosquitoes live longer in humid air, so they have more time to spread the virus.

People and cities

Aedes loves dense cities with many containers and many people to bite.

The delayWeather today affects cases weeks to months later (mosquitoes need time to breed, then the virus needs time to incubate). That is why the model uses weather from 1, 2 and 3 months earlier.

One more thing matters a lot but is invisible to satellites: immunity. After a big outbreak, many people are immune for a while, so the next year can be quiet even with perfect weather. And when a new virus type (serotype) arrives, a huge outbreak can happen. Keep this in mind for the results.

4. The question

Can a model built from satellite, climate and population data predict dengue incidence, and does it still work when moved to a region it was not trained on?

I split this into four smaller questions:

  1. Does it predict future years in the same places? (temporal test)
  2. Does it predict whole regions it never saw? (spatial test)
  3. Does it work in another country? (transfer test, India)
  4. Does it learn things that make biological sense? (explainability)

6. The pipeline (how data flows)

One command, python -m dengue.cli all, runs every step below in order. Each step saves its output, so if anything stops, it picks up where it left off.

1DownloadCases from InfoDengue and OpenDengue, town boundaries from IBGE and DataMeet. Every download is saved with its date.
2Satellite extractionEarth Engine averages each satellite layer over each town, for every month from 2014 to 2026.
3Build the tableWeekly cases become monthly incidence. Weather from 1, 2 and 3 months earlier is attached to each row.
4Train and testBaselines and LightGBM models, tested on future years, unseen regions, and India.
5ExplainSHAP shows which inputs drove each prediction, and checks them against biology.
6PublishPredictions go to a web map and API. A monthly job adds new months.

Turning weeks into months

Brazil reports cases by epidemiological week (Sunday to Saturday). A week can cross two months, so each week is assigned to the month that contains its Wednesday (its middle day). Some months then have 4 weeks and some have 5, which would make 5-week months look 25% worse for no real reason. So counts are scaled to a standard month of 4.345 weeks (52.18 weeks / 12 months).

7. Key concepts (plain language)

Incidence per 100,000

Raw case counts are unfair: a big city always has more cases than a village. So we use cases per 100,000 people. If a town of 50,000 people has 25 cases, its incidence is 25 / 50,000 x 100,000 = 50 per 100,000.

Why log1p (taking the log)

Dengue numbers are wildly uneven: half of all town-months have zero cases, but some have thousands per 100,000 during an epidemic. A model trained on raw numbers would obsess over the few huge outbreaks.

So we predict log1p(incidence) = log(1 + incidence). It squashes big numbers: 0 stays 0, 9 becomes about 2.3, 99 becomes about 4.6, 999 becomes about 6.9. Every 10x increase adds about 2.3. The "+1" lets us handle zeros (the log of 0 is undefined).

All error numbers in this project are on this log scale. An error of 1.0 means the prediction is off by a factor of about 2.7.

Lag (using the past to predict the future)

A "lag 2 temperature" for March is the temperature in January. Because dengue responds to weather with a delay, the model uses weather from 1, 2 and 3 months before. It never uses weather from the month it is predicting. This also means the map can predict next month as soon as this month's weather is published.

Baseline (the "dumb" model to beat)

A model is only impressive if it beats a simple rule. Our main baseline is seasonal climatology: "predict the average this town had in this calendar month in past years". For example, if a town averaged 40 cases per 100,000 in past Marches, predict that for next March. Dengue is very seasonal, so this simple rule is hard to beat.

The second baseline is last year, same month: predict whatever happened 12 months ago.

Data leakage (accidental cheating)

Leakage is when information from the answer sneaks into the inputs. Example: testing on 2024 but using a threshold calculated with 2024 data. The model looks great but would fail for real. This project has automated tests that change all future data and check that no past feature changes, plus tests that training, calibration and test periods never overlap.

Generalization and transfer

Generalization means working on new data. Transfer means working in a new setting: a new region, a new country, a new health system. Transfer is much harder, because the new place can be hotter, higher, more urban, or report cases differently.

Out of range (extrapolation)

A model only knows the world it was trained on. Brazil's highest town averages 1,580 m above sea level. Many Indian Himalayan districts are much higher, and Indian summers are hotter than anything in Brazil. When an input is outside the training range, tree models just reuse the value from the edge of what they saw. The map flags these predictions with a warning.

8. The models

Last year, same month

Copy what happened 12 months ago. Baseline.

LightGBM, climate only

The main model. Uses weather lags, season and fixed land and population features. Never sees past case counts. This is the model on the map.

LightGBM + past cases

Same, plus last 1, 2, 3 and 12 months of cases. Built to show how much "memory" alone explains.

What is LightGBM?

LightGBM is a gradient boosted trees model. Think of it as a team of 800 small decision trees. Each tree asks simple questions like "was last month warmer than 24 degrees? is this town more than 30% built up?" The first tree makes a rough guess, and every next tree tries to fix the mistakes of the trees before it. Together they capture complex patterns, like "warm AND rainy AND dense city means high risk".

It is the standard choice for table data like this: fast, accurate, and handles missing values on its own.

Why two LightGBM versions?

Past cases are extremely predictive (if a town had an outbreak last month, it probably still has one). A model with past cases can score very well without learning anything about climate. Comparing the two versions shows how much is really due to climate. Also, past cases are often not available in real time (reporting is delayed by weeks) and not at all for India districts, so the deployed map uses the climate-only version.

3 seeds

Training has some randomness. Each model is trained 3 times with different random seeds (0, 1, 2), and we report the average. The three runs differed by at most 0.001 in error, so the results are stable.

Uncertainty: a range, not just a number

A single number like "23 cases per 100,000" hides how unsure the model is. So the model also gives a 90% interval, for example "between 0 and 268". It is built in two steps:

  1. Quantile models: two extra LightGBM models predict a low value (5th percentile) and a high value (95th percentile).
  2. Conformal calibration: we check on a held-out year how often reality fell inside that range. If it was inside only 70% of the time, we widen the range until it covers 90%. This is called conformalized quantile regression (CQR).

The catch: this calibration only works if the future looks like the calibration year. Dengue years differ a lot, so coverage is lower than 90% in practice (see results). I report it honestly instead of claiming 90%.

9. How we test (validation)

This is the most important part. A model is only as trustworthy as the test it passed. We use three tests, each harder than the last.

Test 1: Temporal (future years)

Train on the past, test on the future. Time is never shuffled. This mimics real use: you always predict forward.

Test 2: Spatial blocked cross-validation (unseen regions)

Brazil has 5 big regions: North (Amazon), Northeast, Southeast, South and Center-West. We run 5 rounds. In each round, one whole region is hidden, the model trains on the other 4, and then predicts the hidden one. "Blocked" means the hidden region is excluded in both space and time: no rows from it during training at all.

Why this matters: a model can "memorize" places it has seen. Hiding whole regions tests whether it learned something general about climate and dengue.

Test 3: Cross-country transfer (India)

Train on all of Brazil. Predict every Indian district for every month. Add those predictions up to state-year totals. Compare the ranking of states with the real reported ranking. We compare ranks, not exact numbers, because India and Brazil count cases very differently (India under-reports a lot).

Confidence intervals (bootstrap)

Every headline number has a range like 0.012 [0.006, 0.017]. That range is a 95% confidence interval. It is computed by a cluster bootstrap: we redo the score 1,000 times, each time drawing a random sample of towns (with repeats). The spread of those 1,000 scores tells us how much the result could change with different towns. We resample whole towns, not single months, because months from the same town are similar to each other, and treating them as independent would make us look more certain than we are.

Does climate add anything once you know a town's history?

I gave the model each town's seasonal average as an input, then added climate on top. Skill with climate added: -0.008 [-0.010, -0.006]. In plain words: if you already know a town's usual pattern, climate adds nothing for these years.

Test 2: unseen regions

Here, the hidden region has no history at all (that is the point). The only fair baseline is a national seasonal average. Skill of the climate model against it:

What this meansFor a place with no case history, the climate model helps in 4 of 5 regions. That is its real value: a reasonable first guess where surveillance is missing. It struggled in the North (the Amazon), which is very different from the rest of Brazil.

But against the hidden region's own history (which a real health department would have), the model only won in the South. So: use climate where you have no data, and switch to local data as soon as you have it.

Uncertainty check

The 90% intervals actually covered reality 83% of the time on future years, and between 74% and 98% in unseen regions. So the ranges are useful, but not as reliable as "90%" suggests when the world changes.

12. What the model learned (SHAP)

SHAP splits every prediction into contributions from each input. For example: "season raised the risk by +0.63, elevation lowered it by -0.68". Positive pushes the prediction up, negative pushes it down. Averaged over thousands of predictions, it shows what the model relies on.

Temperature: matches biology

Simplified sketch of the real SHAP curve in results/figures/shap_dependence_lgbm_climate.png. Peaks found by code: 25.8, 27.5 and 26.2 degrees C for lags 1, 2 and 3.

The model was never told about mosquito biology, yet it found the same thing lab scientists did: risk rises with warmth, peaks around 26 to 27 degrees C, and drops when it gets too hot.

  • Rain: very dry months lower the risk; moderate rain raises it.
  • Humidity: above about 77% it clearly raises the risk.
  • Most important climate input: mean temperature, at every lag.

Elevation: a warning sign

The single most important input was elevation, and higher meant more dengue. Biology says the opposite (it is colder up high). The reason: in Brazil, the worst dengue areas happen to be on a high central plateau (Goias, Minas Gerais, Sao Paulo), while the low Amazon reports less. So the model learned "high = dengue", which is true in Brazil but not a law of nature. This is called a confounder or proxy. It becomes a real problem in India.

13. The India test

How well did each method rank the 35 Indian states by dengue, averaged over 2015 to 2024?

Honest result: the state ranking failedThe Brazil-trained model ranked Indian states worse than simply using population density. Every year's confidence range included zero.
But the season transferredFor India's national monthly cases in 2024, the model's month-by-month curve matched reality well (Spearman 0.855). It predicted the peak in August; the real peak was October. So it knows when dengue season comes in India, not which states are worst.

Why did it fail? (diagnosis with SHAP)

The model ranked Uttarakhand, a Himalayan state with little dengue, as the riskiest. SHAP showed why: its prediction was driven almost entirely by elevation (mean 1,813 m, higher than any town in Brazil). The "plateau = dengue" pattern from Brazil was being applied to the Himalaya.

Wider check: 24.7% of Indian district-months (in 442 of 641 districts) have at least one input outside what the model saw in Brazil, mostly very high temperatures and elevations.

I then tested a model without elevation. It improved India a little (0.146 to 0.199) but got worse in Brazil (skill 0.012 to -0.014), and still lost to population density. Since I chose this test after seeing the India result, it is reported only as a diagnostic, never as "the fix".

The bigger lessonTransfer is not one thing. Timing transferred, spatial ranking did not. And features that encode one country's geography (elevation, land cover mix) should be checked before moving a model across borders.

16. Engineering choices (and why)

Reproducible

Fixed seeds, pinned package versions, one config file, every download saved with its date. Anyone can rerun and get the same numbers.

No invented numbers

Every number in the README and report is copied from CSVs written by code. A script renders them into tables.

Tested

28 automated tests, including ones that prove future data cannot leak into training features. They run on every push.

Tracked

Every training run is logged to MLflow with its settings and the exact code version.

Cheap to run

Predictions are precomputed, so the API just looks them up. It runs in a small Docker container on a free hosting tier.

Honest by design

Disclaimers on the map itself, extrapolation warnings, and a model card listing limitations.

17. Limitations (what not to trust)

  • Reported cases are not true cases. They depend on testing and reporting, which differ hugely between countries and states.
  • India districts are not validated. No public district case data exists. The district map is relative risk only.
  • Climate cannot see immunity or new virus types, which drive the big year-to-year swings.
  • Intervals cover less than 90% in new years and some regions.
  • Population snapshots: India uses one 2020 population estimate for every year.
  • Data gaps: West Bengal has no records for 2018 and 2019 and a suspiciously low 2024 total.
  • Static features are single snapshots and do not track city growth.

18. Interview Q and A

Tell me about this project in 30 seconds.

I built monthly dengue predictions from public satellite and climate data, trained on Brazil's weekly municipal case data, and tested them on future years, on whole Brazilian regions the model never saw, and on India. The climate model only beats a town's own seasonal average by a hair, but it is a useful first guess for regions with no history. It captured India's seasonal timing but ranked Indian states worse than population density, and SHAP showed why: elevation was a Brazil-specific proxy. The live map shows uncertainty, drivers and data dates, and flags extrapolation.

Why not just report the past-cases model? It scores 0.66.

Because most of that score is persistence: last month's cases predict this month's. It does not prove the model learned climate, it needs case data that is often delayed, and it is impossible for India districts. I report it, but I deploy the climate-only model and say clearly what it can and cannot do.

Why spatial cross-validation and not random splits?

Random splits put neighbouring months and towns in both training and testing, so the model can memorize. Holding out entire regions in both space and time is the closest test to "deploy this somewhere new".

How did you prevent data leakage?

Lags are joined by explicit month offsets, never by row shifting, and only from earlier months. Alert thresholds come from training years only. Tests change all future data and assert that earlier features stay identical, and split tests assert no time or place overlap.

What would you do next?

Add immunity proxies (time since the last big outbreak), test weekly nowcasting, constrain or remove geography proxies before transfer, try per-region calibration of intervals, and validate in a country with district-level data before any district claims in India.

What surprised you?

That the model recovered the lab-measured temperature optimum without being told any biology, and that the most important feature, elevation, was the one that broke the transfer.
